Research is often described as a private cognitive act followed by public
communication. I increasingly find the reverse more useful: learning happens
inside artifacts that are already social. A note, a dataset, a model card, or a
small proof is not merely the residue of thinking. It is the place where thinking
becomes inspectable.

The modern machine learning paper makes this visible. The transformer is not
just an architecture; it is a dense bundle of mathematical notation, experimental
convention, code, diagrams, and citations [@vaswani2017attention]. Once the
bundle is public, other people can stress it, extend it, and teach with it.

For a minimal example, consider attention weights written as
$\operatorname{softmax}(QK^\top / \sqrt{d_k})V$. The expression matters because
it is compact, but its usefulness also depends on the practices around it: how
we name tensors, how we compare baselines, and how we record failure.

$$
\mathrm{Attention}(Q, K, V) =
\operatorname{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V
$$

This is why I want a personal site to behave less like a portfolio and more like
a working shelf. Some pages should be finished essays. Some should remain
careful sketches. Others should simply record a question well enough that I can
return to it with better tools.

## Notes as Instruments

Latour's account of science emphasizes inscriptions, chains of reference, and
the work required to make claims travel [@latour1987science]. Digital writing
systems inherit the same burden. If a note cannot carry its sources, assumptions,
and revisions with it, it quickly becomes decorative memory.

The aim, then, is modest: write in a way that keeps reasoning portable. A useful
research note should make three things easy to recover:

- what question was being asked;
- which evidence changed the shape of the question;
- what would make the note obsolete.

That is a small standard, but it is enough to make writing more than storage.
